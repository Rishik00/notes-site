# SOAP, Muon, and Beyond: Pushing LLM Pretraining Scales

## One-line takeaway

1. They prove muon scales nicer and is also stable at higher batch sizes with some empirical experiments.&#x20;
2. They attempt at stabilizing SOAP using KL minimization.&#x20;

## Problem

Second order optimizers like Muon, SOAP, Shampoo are awesome! But they're also very hard to tune and do not consistently produce stable models when used in training compared to first order optimizers like AdamW.&#x20;

## Core idea

1. Conduct a update-RMS study between Muon, AdamW and SOAP for multi billion param pretraining runs (30B, \~600M).&#x20;
2. Fix SOAP's preconditioner update instabilities using KL minimization.
3. Introduce a layer-wise-optimizer that's compatible with Megatron LM

## Method

They train many 4 core architectures - nemotron-3-30B-A3B, 8B Dense transformer, Nemotron-3-72b-a8b, Qwen3-30b-a3b with seq\_len\=8192 with a step-wise batch size schedule (from 50-75M tokens).&#x20;

Core optimizers to be compared are Muon, SOAP and AdamW. They adapt something called the update-RMS matching framework from kimi that allows to transfer LRs effectively between optimizers by ensuring the RMS norm of parameter updates is consistent across methods, reducing tuning compute.&#x20;

For SOAP, they adapt the preconditioner updates using KL minimization to ensure that the eigenbasis updates don't blow out of proportion. They claim:&#x20;

> This empirical improvement comes from better estimation of the gradient covariance, and the stability benefit comes from the spectral properties of the update.

They attribute SOAP's instability to a "slingshot" behavior. The loss landscape changes rapidly during the early stages of large batch training, applying a preconditioning matrix derived from outdated gradient statistics causes the trajectory to slingshot. (SOAP/Shampoo/KFAC apply preconditioners every n-steps for compute efficiency and stability, but papers also note that these n-step applications drop earlier gradient statistics).&#x20;

## Results

1. When scaling batch sizes between AdamW and Muon, they observed that AdamW quickly hits a ceiling while muon continues to scale and give better results. They hypothesize this behavior to Adam's estimator getting more and more confident as it sees less noisy gradient updates (the rule of thumb with batch size scaling is that higher batch sizes give better approximate gradient updates)
2. Muon does end up providing with much stabler loss curves than AdamW for dense, MoE and hybrids (using mamba) at different scales of batch sizes. They also validated these results in MXFP8, which is cool (idk what MXFP8 is).
3. Downstream perf-wise, muon with batch size scaling beats AdamW in every conceivable way. The margin is not that wide, but the fact that a second order optimizer that accounts for curvature information is on par with the most widely used first order optimizer on similar settings is really cool!&#x20;
4. They also do SOAP vs Muon runs on the same architectures, and find that the KL penalty reduces the spikes in loss and brings them closer in terms of stability. They don't report downstream metrics for this run.&#x20;

## Limitations

1. It's not a limitation per-se, more of a comment: they don't really talk about compute or wall clock time in their results at all. If the second order optimizers are getting us there but do it slightly faster than they did before that's awesome.&#x20;
2. They don't talk about KFAC variants at all. It'd be nice if they followed up or someone followed up with KFAC variants.&#x20;
3. They do give hypotheses, but don't necessarily back them with evidence sometimes (see Follow up questions #1).&#x20;
