# Attention Drift: What Autoregressive Speculative Decoding Models Learn

## One-line takeaway

With a tiny change, they solved the issue of attention drift in EAGLE-3 drafters.&#x20;

## Problem

The drafter just inherits the same attention drift problem that the verifier has. This also includes inheriting the attention drift from the verifier.&#x20;

The authors hypothesize that this makes the drafter more fragile to out of distribution inputs.&#x20;

## Core idea

This is an analysis paper that hopes to uncover the reason behind such a phenomenon. Their core insight was to move the RMSNorm before extracting the hidden states and that seems to have prevented the problem.

## Method and Results

> TLDR: They checked for attention patterns during speculative decoding in verifiers and EAGLE-3, MTP trained drafters. 

### Insights

1. MTP head of Qwen 3.5 9B showcases this behaviour: the drafter's attention sinsk shift from the early tokens to more recent tokens.&#x20;
2. When checked on EAGLE-3 drafters of models such as Qwen-3 Next or GPT-OSS, the sinks still seem to be recurring but with a different pattern. This suggests that the behaviour is inherited from the parent model.&#x20;
3. The magnitude of hidden states for many architectures (Llama 8B, Qwen 30B and 35B, GPT OSS 20 and 120B) was checked, and it was found that it shows the same trend (from low--> medium---> high layers) as the drafter's hidden states at various rounds.
4. The authors note the relationship between drift and attention for the drafter as the result of similar looking hidden states as generation progresses which shifts the attention mass away from intial tokens to current tokens.&#x20;

### The verification&#x20;

They further trained drafters with slight variations to find the root cause of the problem. They claim that the un normalised structure of the drafter model makes it so that the drafter just acts like additional transformer layers than be an independent autoregressive model.&#x20;

Two variations of this were performed:&#x20;

1. Pre norm vs post norm, the age old debate. They find that post norm drafters trained with reasonable depths are stable.&#x20;
2. Deep vs shallow drafters, specifically to observe if the models have learned a depth invariant transformations. But this turns out to be false.&#x20;

See, try to understand why they even thought of this. Their earlier hidden state magnitude experiment suggested that the magnitudes and sinks might be correlated, and they found that drafters are pre-norm. So by ablating on that, they arrived at a solution: do **post-norm.**&#x20;

problem solved right? Not really. To understand the improvements in real time they deployed 4 drafters: (1) pre-norm (2) gated attention (3) post-norm and (4) gated + post norm.&#x20;

They found the following:&#x20;

1. Gated attention drafter doesn't have any sinks at all, but it's hidden state magnitude grows considerably from round 1-->8.&#x20;
2. post norm pins the hidden state magnitudes across speculation steps and stabilizes attention pattern over the drafting chain. But sink still exists.&#x20;
3. Post norm drafters also apparently sustain a lot more noise than pre-norm, which is important for EAGLE-3 style drafters because they are autoregressive drafters.&#x20;

These insights reveal to us that while attention sinks do remove drifting, it doesn't remove the magnitudes problem. Ultimately they also find these gains to transfer to other architectures under EAGLE-3 and also show better acceptance rates.&#x20;

## Limitations

1. A large focus was towards EAGLE-3. We don't know if these changes transfer to other feature level speculative decoding methods.&#x20;

> **How is drift and magnitude related?**

As the drafter generates deeper into the speculation chain, its current hidden state increasingly resembles recently generated drafter states rather than verifier-conditioned prompt states. The empirical consequence, quantified in the next section, is a redistribution of attention mass away from the original input and onto the drafter’s own predictions.
