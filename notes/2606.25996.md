# Autodata: An agentic data scientist to create high quality synthetic data

## One-line takeaway

Cool concept. But I think it's under tested and is capable of much more than this.

## Problem

Constructing loops for generating synthetic data has always been a challenge, and involves very complex systems to do so.&#x20;

## Core idea

Autodata is an agentic synthetic data library that was constructed with the agentic self-instruct protocol. The core idea is to show a judge what qualifies as a good example from a strong agent and a weak agent.&#x20;

## Method

The agentic self instruct protocol has 4 components:

1. **The main agent**, that takes a prompt and some grounding data (afaik)
2. **Challenger**, which creates training examples given a good prompt from the main agent.&#x20;
3. **Strong solver**, that generally is assumed to do well for the task at hand.&#x20;
4. **Weak solver** that is expected to struggle during the process.&#x20;
5. **Verifier/judge** that checks quality, and passes a report of feedback to the main agent.&#x20;

They apply this architecture to 3 areas, generating synthetic data and RLing Qwen 3.5 4B (GRPO):

1. Scientific reasoning (cool shit, not covered in this note) - taking scientific benchmark and curating synthetic data for it.&#x20;
2. Legal (skipped in this note) - idk what they do in this but it sounded&#x20;
3. CS research - pick a paper, generate QA pairs.&#x20;

## Results

* On CS research, they generated roughly 1.3k examples and compare the GRPO performance against an existing dataset and dataset generated by CoT self instruct. It outperforms both significantly.&#x20;
* On deeper analysis, it was found that the agents were spending the first couple of iterations just writing summary QA pairs which required lots of feedback from the judge. The weak solver started off strong but slowly dropped in perf because the questions kept getting better and better. &#x20;

## Limitations

* The choice of strong and weak agent means everything for this. For experiments they can bias themselves and use trashy models to "influence" the data (sort of).&#x20;
