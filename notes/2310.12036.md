# A General Theoretical Paradigm to Understand Learning from Human Preferences

## One-line takeaway

Oh shit, this is the IPO paper (identity preference optimization) and it's insanely cool (and complicated).

## Problem

Apparently there's no general theory describing learning algorithms that consider human preferences (RLHF).

And DPO is also prone to overfitting, which causes problems.

## Core idea

They write a unifying/generalized theory for any preference optimization algorithm and called it ΨPO where Ψ can be anything.

## Method

### Part-1: The theory

They start with stating the notation, if you really want to understand the paper please memorize this because it gets lengthy:

* **y, y'**: two complete responses generated for a prompt. y\_w and y\_l are the same pair but labeled — y\_w is the one the human preferred (winner), y\_l is the one they didn't (loser).
* **p\\\*(y ≻ y' | x)**: the true probability that a human prefers response y over response y', given prompt x. p\\\*(y ≻ μ | x) \= E\_{y'∼μ}\[p\\\*(y ≻ y' | x)] is how often y beats a random draw from μ.
* x: the prompt/context.
* **π(y|x) and π\_ref(y|x)**: the policy being trained and the frozen reference policy (usually the SFT model), respectively.
* **I(y, y')**: binary indicator — 1 if y was preferred over y', 0 otherwise.
* **Ψ**: a general non-decreasing mapping function that transforms preference probabilities. Different choices of Ψ give different algorithms.

Now, we define the generalized preference optimization objective as:

$$
\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x \sim \rho \\ y \sim \pi(\cdot|x) \\ y' \sim \mu(\cdot|x)}} [\Psi(p^*(y \succ y' | x))] - \tau D_{\text{KL}}(\pi \| \pi_{\text{ref}})
$$

Then they state that the above equation can be further derived to DPO's objective via substituting Ψ \= log(q/(1−q)) (the logit / inverse sigmoid), which gives:

$$
\Psi(p^*(y \succ y')) = \log\frac{\sigma(r(y) - r(y'))}{1 - \sigma(r(y) - r(y'))} = r(y) - r(y')
$$

The logit and sigmoid cancel each other, collapsing the preference term down to the raw reward difference. Under expectation over y', this becomes r(y) − E\_{y'∼μ}\[r(y')], which is the RLHF objective up to a constant.

They also derive a very useful relationship between π(y) and π\_ref(y) from the above equation which is:

$$
\pi^*(y) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y) \exp\left(\tau^{-1} \mathbb{E}_{y' \sim \mu}[\Psi(p^*(y \succ y'))]\right)
$$

### Part-2: Regularisation and a new loss function

The authors further state that the KL divergence term is very sensitive to rewards that are high in magnitude. They push the necessity for a loss function/objective that is more bounded than the sigmoid term that DPO uses.

To make things simpler, they remove the log sigmoid term entirely and propose a new objective:

$$
\max_\pi \; p^*_\rho(\pi \succ \mu) - \tau D_{\text{KL}}(\pi \| \pi_{\text{ref}})
$$

There's only one problem, such an objective is dependent on the true probabilities in theory p\\\*(y ≻ μ) which is...not computable. So we do the loss derivation.

We first form a simplified version of the current objective as:

$$
h_\pi(y, y') = \tau^{-1}(p^*(y \succ \mu) - p^*(y' \succ \mu))
$$

which gives us the loss:

$$
L(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \mu}\left[\left(h_\pi(y, y') - \frac{p^*(y \succ \mu) - p^*(y' \succ \mu)}{\tau}\right)^2\right]
$$

Where:

$$
h_\pi(y, y') = \log\frac{\pi(y)\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y')}{\pi(y')\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}
$$

The above equation is further supported by a theorem that states there's a perfect policy π\\\* that is able to achieve near zero loss (theoretically) which means that it's able to approximate its log probability ratios to match the true probabilities in preferences.

Now that we know such a loss term exists, it's still not computable. So now we define a more empirical version of this:

$$
L(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim \mu}\left[\left(h_\pi(y, y') - \tau^{-1} I(y, y')\right)^2\right]
$$

Reason for this to exist? The I(y, y') is here to empirically approximate the true probabilities wrt to a dataset μ. They then prove that the 2 equations (13, 16) are equivalent plus a constant. This is necessary because we have to make sure we're not computing the wrong thing here.

Now that we know equation 16 is tractable, let's simplify it even further. We know that for every sample in the dataset (y\_w, y\_l, I(y, y')) we get this:

When y\_w is more preferred to y\_l, I \= 1:

$$
(h_\pi(y_w, y_l) - \tau^{-1})^2
$$

When y\_l is more preferred to y\_w (reversed pair), I \= 0:

$$
h_\pi(y_l, y_w)^2 = (-h_\pi(y_w, y_l))^2 = h_\pi(y_w, y_l)^2
$$

Averaging and completing the square gives us the final term:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{IPO}}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{(y_w, y_l) \sim D}\left[\left(h_\pi(y_w, y_l) - \frac{1}{2\tau}\right)^2\right]
$$

is the final version of the IPO loss function.

### Part-3: Experiments

They test it on a bandit problem. Yes, they derived everything for a contextual RL problem like language modelling and for some reason thought it was a good idea to test this thing for a problem where context doesn't matter at all.
